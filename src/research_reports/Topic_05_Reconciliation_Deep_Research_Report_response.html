Manuscript Transcription Reconciliation Methods

Reconciliation and Metrics in Digital Philology: Methods for Reconciling Manual, HTR, and AI-Generated Manuscript Transcriptions

1. Abstract and Executive Summary

The proliferation of mass digitization initiatives over the past two decades has fundamentally transformed the landscape of textual scholarship, yet it has simultaneously precipitated a profound epistemological crisis regarding textual reliability. As historical manuscripts and early printed books are translated into machine-readable formats through manual double-keying, Optical Character Recognition (OCR), and Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR), the resulting digital surrogates frequently harbor pervasive transcription noise. Establishing a definitive consensus text from these disparate and often conflicting data streams requires rigorous algorithmic reconciliation, sophisticated statistical benchmarking, and meticulously designed post-correction workflows. This comprehensive report investigates the precise methodologies and mathematical metrics that govern the evaluation, reconciliation, and subsequent post-correction of digital transcriptions in the context of advanced digital humanities and computational philology.

The core thesis advanced herein posits that systematic text reconciliation—achieved through double-keying error analysis, Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) benchmarking, and robust inter-annotator agreement statistical modeling—is absolutely essential for quantifying the efficacy of automated transcription pipelines. Without such empirical verification, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) into scholarly editing risks introducing unquantifiable systematic biases, historical anachronisms, and textual hallucinations into the scholarly record. By evaluating the mathematical foundations of the Levenshtein distance and the Wagner-Fischer algorithm, this analysis elucidates how raw string similarities are computed and utilized to identify typographical versus structural transcription errors. Furthermore, the synthesis explores the critical threshold at which transcription noise fatally degrades downstream Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, demonstrating the non-linear decay of algorithmic comprehension when confronted with historical textual artifacts.

Through exhaustive examinations of large-scale, real-world initiatives—including the Deutsches Textarchiv (DTA), the IMPACT project, the Bullinger Letters alignment project, and recent advancements in Pre-Editorial Normalization (PEN) for medieval manuscripts—this report demonstrates how targeted post-correction workflows bridge the usability gap between graphemic fidelity and normalized editorial expectations. Ultimately, the analysis translates these highly technical methodologies into pedagogical frameworks, equipping future digital philologists with the theoretical and practical competencies necessary to construct, audit, and mathematically verify digital scholarly editions.

2. Technical, Methodological, and Historical Foundations

The Double-Keying Methodology

In the pursuit of establishing virtually error-free digital texts, particularly for historical documents characterized by complex layouts, obsolete typography (such as early modern Fraktur fonts), and highly irregular orthography, the double-keying methodology remains the gold standard of mass digitization. The process mandates that two independent human operators manually transcribe the identical source text. The resulting divergent textual outputs are subsequently compared via computational string-matching algorithms to identify discrepancies, which a senior editor or an automated reconciliation script then adjudicates to form a final consensus text.  

The epistemological strength of double-keying derives from the statistical improbability of two independent operators committing the exact same typographical error at the precise same character position. Commercial data entry services employing this method routinely guarantee accuracy rates oscillating between 99.95% and 99.98%. Furthermore, stringent institutional benchmarks, such as the German Research Foundation (DFG) Practical Guidelines on Digitization, stipulate an accuracy threshold of 99.997%, yielding a digitized corpus that is theoretically "virtually error-free". A profound methodological insight regarding double-keying is the frequent deployment of non-native speakers to perform the primary transcription. By deliberately severing the transcriber's semantic comprehension of the text from the visual recognition of its graphemes, the methodology effectively neutralizes the psychological phenomenon of auto-correction, wherein a native speaker might unconsciously modernize a historical spelling variant or mentally insert an omitted character.  

Evaluating the ultimate correctness of double-keying necessitates a strict bipartite classification of errors: typographical and structural. Typographical errors occur at the character level, encompassing omissions, substitutions, and insertions of textual content. Conversely, structural errors manifest in the Extensible Markup Language (XML) or Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) markup layer, representing a failure to accurately capture the semantic or physical architecture of the document—for example, misidentifying a marginal note as a primary paragraph, or misapplying a speaker tag in a dramatic text. The rigorous separation of these error classes is vital; typographical fidelity is rendered meaningless if the structural scaffolding of the XML renders the text computationally unreadable to parsing algorithms.  

Levenshtein Distance and the Wagner-Fischer Algorithm

The mathematical reconciliation of differing text streams—whether comparing two divergent manual transcriptions, or benchmarking an HTR output against an established ground truth—relies predominantly on edit distance metrics, most prominently the Levenshtein distance. Formulated by Soviet mathematician Vladimir Levenshtein in 1965, this metric mathematically quantifies the dissimilarity between two sequences by calculating the absolute minimum number of single-character editing operations required to transform the source string into the target string. The permissible operations are strictly restricted to insertions, deletions, and substitutions.  

While the recursive mathematical definition of the Levenshtein distance is robust, its direct computational implementation is exponentially inefficient, exhibiting overlapping subproblems that drastically slow processing times for long textual sequences. This inefficiency was definitively resolved by the Wagner-Fischer algorithm (1974), which applies a bottom-up dynamic programming approach to compute the edit distance in O(mn) time and space complexity, where m and n denote the absolute lengths of the two strings being compared.  

The Wagner-Fischer algorithm initializes a computational matrix D of dimensions (m+1)×(n+1). A foundational necessity of this matrix, which often confuses novice programmers, is the inclusion of the empty string (representing a prefix of length zero) as the base case for both the source and target strings. This initialization step is mathematically imperative because it establishes the baseline cost of transforming any given string into an empty string (requiring i consecutive deletions) and transforming an empty string into any target string (requiring j consecutive insertions). The algorithm populates the matrix according to the following state transition logic, evaluating the optimal sub-path for every character:  

D[i,j]=min⎩⎨⎧​D[i−1,j]+1D[i,j−1]+1D[i−1,j−1]+cost​(Deletion)(Insertion)(Substitution)​

In this formulation, the substitution cost equals 0 if the characters s[i] and t[j] are identical; otherwise, the cost equals 1. Upon traversing the entire sequence, the integer residing in the bottom-right cell of the matrix, D[m,n], represents the absolute Levenshtein distance. The underlying trend demonstrated by this dynamic programming approach is the spatial representation of textual mutation; by tracing the optimal path backward from D[m,n] to D[0,0], digital philologists can systematically extract the precise alignment of typographical errors, categorizing exactly where an HTR engine hallucinated a character, or where a human transcriber skipped a word during the double-keying process.  

Mathematical Definitions of CER and WER

The raw Levenshtein distance informs the two most critical benchmark metrics in digital document analysis: Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER). These metrics normalize the raw edit distance against the total length of the ground truth text, providing a comparable percentage of transcription inaccuracy across texts of highly variable lengths.  

The Character Error Rate is defined mathematically by the formula:

CER=NS+D+I​

Where S represents the total number of character substitutions, D represents character deletions, I represents character insertions, and N represents the total number of characters in the verified reference ground truth sequence. Because insertions are added to the numerator without a corresponding increase in the denominator N, it is mathematically possible for the CER to exceed 100% in instances of severe algorithmic hallucination, wherein an HTR model generates vast strings of garbage text from a degraded manuscript image.  

The Word Error Rate utilizes the identical mathematical formula, but shifts the unit of analysis from the individual character to the token (typically defined as whitespace-delimited words). WER is traditionally much higher than CER because a single incorrect character within a twenty-character word renders the entire word mathematically incorrect under the Wagner-Fischer evaluation. A deep examination of the relationship between CER and WER reveals a fundamental dynamic in HTR evaluation: an automated model that produces a low CER but a disproportionately high WER is almost certainly suffering from systemic boundary detection failures, arbitrarily inserting whitespace and fracturing cohesive lexical units into nonsensical fragments.  

Inter-Annotator Agreement Metrics

While CER and WER evaluate the divergence between a transcription hypothesis and a verified ground truth, establishing that ground truth originally requires reconciling the subjective judgments of human annotators. When multiple philologists are tasked with tagging named entities, resolving complex paleographic abbreviations, or defining structural TEI boundaries, their conformity must be statistically validated to ensure that the dataset is not corrupted by idiosyncratic interpretation.  

The simplest measurement of agreement—joint probability or percentage agreement—is structurally flawed because it completely fails to account for instances where annotators agree purely by random chance. To rectify this, researchers utilize chance-adjusted agreement metrics, prominently the Kappa family of statistics and Krippendorff's Alpha.

Cohen's Kappa evaluates the agreement of exactly two raters. The value Po​ denotes the relative observed agreement among the raters, while Pe​ computes the hypothetical probability of chance agreement based on the marginal distributions of the raters' distinct categorizations. A value of 1 indicates perfect alignment, while 0 indicates no agreement beyond what would be expected by random chance. However, Cohen's Kappa suffers from paradoxes regarding prevalence and bias; in manuscript corpora where a specific phenomenon is exceedingly rare (e.g., a highly specific scribal abbreviation), high observed agreement can paradoxically result in a low Kappa score because the highly skewed marginal distributions inflate the mathematical expectation of chance agreement.  

For large-scale transcription efforts involving crowd-sourced labor or multiple scholarly annotators, Fleiss' Kappa extends the chance-adjusted metric to accommodate more than two raters. Yet, the most robust metric for digital humanities and textual editing remains Krippendorff's Alpha. Unlike the Kappa statistics, which calculate observed versus expected agreement, Krippendorff's Alpha calculates observed versus expected disagreement. Furthermore, it seamlessly handles missing data—a frequent occurrence in illegible historical manuscripts where one annotator may simply skip a damaged word that another attempts to transcribe—and supports various distance metrics across nominal, ordinal, and continuous data structures.  

3. Comparative Methodological Evaluation

Reconciling Human Disagreement vs. Scoring HTR and LLM Output

The reconciliation of human-generated double-keying output operates on fundamentally different epistemological premises than the scoring of automated HTR or Large Language Model (LLM) outputs. When two human transcribers disagree, the error topology is heavily weighted toward cognitive substitutions (e.g., misreading a long 's' for an 'f' due to paleographic similarities) or simple mechanical transposition errors resulting from keyboard layout (e.g., typing "teh" instead of "the"). In these scenarios, the consensus text is established through targeted manual arbitration, often relying on the aforementioned inter-annotator agreement metrics to identify which specific transcriber is consistently deviating from the physical paleographic reality of the document.

Conversely, the error typology generated by sequence-to-sequence neural networks, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and LLMs interacting with manuscript images introduces distinct, systemic algorithmic artifacts. Automated HTR models frequently struggle with macro-structural preservation, exhibiting extremely high insertion and deletion rates at the physical boundaries of text lines. A persistent anomaly in historical text recognition is the handling of line-end hyphenation; predictive models frequently fail to reconstruct the fractured lexical unit, treating the hyphenated fragment at the end of one line and the beginning of the next as distinct, unrelated tokens, thereby artificially inflating the Word Error Rate and destroying the semantic coherence of the sentence.  

Furthermore, AI-generated transcriptions are highly susceptible to out-of-vocabulary (OOV) failures. If a historical manuscript contains a unique geographical toponym or an idiosyncratic spelling variant not comprehensively represented in the HTR model's training data, the predictive algorithm will often substitute the anomalous word with a visually similar but linguistically incorrect word that possesses a higher probability distribution within the model's lexicon. This phenomenon highlights a critical distinction: whereas human transcribers transcribe what they see (even if it makes no immediate semantic sense), AI models transcribe what they statistically expect to see, introducing pervasive anachronisms into the text.  

The Impact of Transcription Noise on Downstream NLP

The imperative to aggressively drive down CER and WER through rigorous post-correction is not merely an exercise in typographical pedantry; transcription noise exerts a devastating and highly non-linear impact on downstream computational analysis. A comprehensive extrinsic assessment of OCR quality impact conducted by van Strien et al. (2020) definitively demonstrated that the ingestion of noisy historical texts irrevocably harms standard Natural Language Processing (NLP) tools, which are overwhelmingly trained on pristine, modern digital corpora.  

The degradation of these tools follows disparate trajectories based on the specific nature of the computational task. Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging exhibits a relatively linear decay; the tagger's accuracy degrades in direct proportion to the error rate of the OCR output, crossing a threshold of significant uselessness at approximately 5% CER. The degradation occurs primarily because morphological suffixes, which POS taggers heavily rely upon to determine syntactic categories (e.g., identifying a verb by an "-ed" suffix), are the elements most frequently corrupted by character-level substitutions during the OCR process.  

The impact on Named Entity Recognition (NER), however, represents a catastrophic failure cascade. The primary function of NER is to extract proper nouns—such as individuals, geographical locations, and organizations—from the surrounding prose. Named entities inherently exhibit low textual redundancy; if the name "Robespierre" is misspelled as "Kobespierre" due to a degraded capital letter on the manuscript, the entire entity is permanently lost to the search index. Empirical evidence from van Strien's evaluations indicates that NER F-scores drop precipitously from an acceptable 90% down to a mathematically useless 60% when the WER of the text increases from 1% to just 7%, or when the CER increases from 8% to 20%. The underlying cause is that named entities frequently overlap with the precise out-of-vocabulary terms that automated recognition models struggle to process. Consequently, without human-in-the-loop post-correction or specialized algorithmic reconciliation, the extraction of historical actors and geographic networks from noisy digitizations is statistically invalid.  

Pre-Editorial Normalization and LLM Output Scoring

To bridge the usability gap between raw, noisy HTR output and the pristine texts required by downstream NLP, contemporary digital philology has introduced the paradigm of Pre-Editorial Normalization (PEN). Explored extensively by Clérice et al. (2026) in the context of medieval Latin and Old French manuscripts, the PEN task is explicitly designed to algorithmically normalize graphemic ATR (Automatic Text Recognition) output according to standardized editorial conventions before the text is subjected to final human curation.  

Historically, textual scholarship has existed on a spectrum between the strictly graphetic (recording every minute visual variation, retaining unexpanded abbreviations, and mirroring the exact physical layout) and the heavily normalized (expanding all abbreviations, modernizing capitalization, and standardizing punctuation). Graphemic models, trained on highly specialized datasets like CATMuS or CoMMA, preserve historical variations and demonstrate high generalizability across disparate manuscript hands, but yield output that fundamentally breaks modern NLP tools.  

The implementation of LLMs, specifically byte-level sequence-to-sequence transformers like ByT5, addresses this gap by ingesting the graphemic HTR output and generating a pre-edited consensus text. The LLM is instructed via complex few-shot prompting to resolve scribal abbreviations contextually (e.g., expanding a nasal macron into an 'm' or an 'n' depending on the ensuing consonant), normalize the ramist letters ('u' versus 'v', 'i' versus 'j') based strictly on vocalic or consonantal usage, and rectify standard HTR confusions (e.g., distinguishing a minim sequence parsed as 'rn' versus 'm').  

However, evaluating LLM output requires intense scholarly vigilance against the phenomenon of algorithmic over-normalization and hallucination. When LLMs process historical texts characterized by fluid orthography, they frequently collapse historically significant dialectical variants into a singular standard form, thereby permanently erasing critical linguistic evidence. Furthermore, LLMs may hallucinate entire strings of content when encountering severe HTR noise, seamlessly generating grammatically coherent but entirely fabricated prose to bridge the illegible gap. Therefore, the reconciliation of LLM-generated transcriptions mandates strict boundaries: abbreviations corresponding to proper names lacking contextual clarity must remain unexpanded, and continuous spans of severe noise must be flagged for human review rather than algorithmically overwritten. Utilizing these constraints, Clérice et al. demonstrated that a tightly controlled ByT5 normalization model achieved a robust 6.7% CER on medieval manuscripts, substantially accelerating the subsequent human editorial workflow without sacrificing paleographic integrity.  

4. Real-World Digital Edition Case Studies

Case Study 1: The Deutsches Textarchiv (DTA)

The Deutsches Textarchiv (DTA), hosted by the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences and Humanities (BBAW), serves as a paramount case study in the deployment of mass double-keying for the creation of an unimpeachable historical reference corpus. Digitizing printed works in the New High German Language dating from 1600 to 1900, the DTA elected to bypass OCR entirely for its foundational texts. This decision acknowledged the severe limitations of optical recognition on early modern Fraktur fonts, irregular inking, and highly variable historic spelling patterns. By January 2016, the archive comprised 2,331 texts representing approximately 1.1 billion characters.  

To establish its consensus texts, the DTA outsourced the primary transcription to non-native speakers executing a strict double-keying protocol. The vendors guaranteed a minimum character accuracy of 99.9%. Following the initial algorithmic reconciliation of the dual data streams, the DTA applied extensive linguistic analysis and rule-based heuristics to normalize historic spellings to modern lemmas, enabling advanced phonetic and lemma-based search capabilities across the corpus.  

Crucially, the DTA's methodological approach highlights the absolute necessity of distinguishing the typographical layer from the semantic markup layer. All transcriptions were mapped to the DTA "Base Format" (DTABf), a highly customized, strict subset of the TEI P5 guidelines designed to enforce standardized structural markup across massive and heterogeneous text collections. Haaf, Wiegand, and Geyken demonstrated in their statistical analyses that while the typographical error rate of the double-keying process was vanishingly small, structural errors—such as the incorrect demarcation of a marginal note, or the misapplication of a speaker tag in a drama—required sophisticated XML validation schemas (e.g., Schematron) and continuous community-driven quality control platforms to resolve. The eventual evolution of the DTABf to the DTABf-M (Manuscripts) further necessitated the expansion of the TEI subset to accommodate the unique physical architectures of handwritten documents, proving that transcription accuracy is inseparable from structural encoding accuracy.  

Case Study 2: The IMPACT Project and the Australian Newspaper Digitisation Program (ANDP)

The digitisation of historical newspapers presents uniquely hostile conditions for automated transcription. Degrading paper quality, complex multi-column layouts, inter-article boundary failures, and highly variable typography render traditional OCR algorithms highly inaccurate. During the initial phases of the Australian Newspaper Digitisation Program (ANDP), raw OCR accuracy on historical newspapers ranged radically from 71% to 98%, falling well below the operational threshold necessary for reliable full-text searching.  

While the DTA solved accuracy deficits through comprehensive manual double-keying, the massive scale of newspaper archives rendered this approach financially prohibitive. The IMPACT project estimated the cost of manual keying at approximately 1 EUR per page, meaning a single large archival volume could easily consume upwards of 1,000 EUR in transcription costs alone. To achieve a functional consensus text while mitigating costs, the ANDP pioneered a targeted hybrid methodology. Contractors were utilized to manually correct only the critical structural and metadata elements of the newspaper—specifically the article titles, subtitles, and the first four lines of text—guaranteeing a 99.5% accuracy rate for these high-value fields.  

For the vast expanse of the remaining body text, the program instituted a revolutionary crowd-sourced post-correction model through the Trove platform. By exposing the raw, noisy OCR output to the public alongside the scanned image, volunteers manually reconciled the text line-by-line. Complementing this human effort, institutions frequently deploy Recognizer Output Voting for Error Reduction (ROVER) techniques. ROVER aligns the hypotheses of multiple differing OCR engines operating on the same image, utilizing majority voting algorithms and dynamic programming to select the most probable correct character at any given position, thereby organically driving down the WER prior to human intervention. The profound structural insight derived from the ANDP and IMPACT project is that a consensus text in digital philology does not necessarily need to be achieved prior to publication; rather, the digital edition can function as a dynamic, living document that asymptotically approaches perfection through continuous, decentralized human post-correction.  

Case Study 3: Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) Alignment in the Bullinger Letters

The Bullinger dataset, a vast collection of 3,622 16th-century letters written in Latin and premodern German by the Swiss reformer Heinrich Bullinger, presented a unique reconciliation challenge. While expert historians had produced highly accurate transcriptions of the letters, these transcriptions existed as continuous paragraphs devoid of spatial layout information, rendering them useless for training layout-aware HTR systems. When automated systems like Transkribus were used to align these transcriptions with the physical line images, it introduced systematic hyphenation errors, fracturing words that spanned across line breaks.  

To resolve this, Peer, Scius-Bertrand, and Fischer (2025) introduced a self-training methodology utilizing a Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) alignment algorithm. The algorithm ingested the expert-provided, layout-free transcription and the character posterior probabilities generated by HTR models (including PyLaia, HTRFlor, and Retsinas models). Utilizing dynamic programming, the CTC algorithm found the optimal sequence to insert line breaks into the continuous transcription, perfectly matching the visual layout of the scanned pages.  

The implementation revealed a fascinating paradox: the alignment accuracy was frequently higher when utilizing a weakly trained HTR model rather than a highly optimized one, as the weaker model had not yet overfit to the faulty, hyphen-broken training data. By iteratively filtering the alignments based on a confidence threshold (γ6​, measuring the confidence of the first and last six characters of a line) and retraining the models, the researchers successfully improved the CER of the PyLaia model by 1.1 percentage points. This case study demonstrates that establishing a consensus text is not merely about correcting characters, but about perfectly reconciling the semantic transcription with the physical, geospatial coordinates of the manuscript page.  

Case Study 4: Multimodal LLM Extraction from Herbarium Specimens

The reconciliation of historical scientific data presents challenges that extend beyond prose. In the digitisation of botanical herbarium specimens, the "text" consists of highly unstructured, multi-modal elements: the physical pressed plant, scale bars, color charts, and degraded, handwritten taxonomic labels. Standard OCR engines fail completely when attempting to parse the erratic spatial distribution of these labels.  

To establish a verified digital record from the UoS_Data_Rescue dataset, researchers implemented a sophisticated multi-stage AI pipeline. First, an object detection model isolated the physical label from the plant matter. Second, a transformer-based OCR model (TrOCR) generated a raw transcription of the handwritten text. Recognizing the extreme vulnerability of TrOCR to out-of-vocabulary taxonomic names and archaic abbreviations, the researchers introduced the ByT5 byte-level sequence-to-sequence model as an automated post-correction layer.  

Because ByT5 operates at the byte level rather than relying on pre-defined token vocabularies, it successfully reconstructed highly complex, misspelled, and archaic Latin binomials that standard LLMs would have hallucinogenically "corrected" into common modern words. The reconciliation of these labels into a consensus database record required the AI to actively integrate the visual features of the leaf morphology with the transcribed text, relying on contrastive learning models to ensure that the transcribed species name logically matched the visual data of the specimen.  

5. Pedagogical Takeaways and Synthesis for Digital Editors

Integrating these complex, multi-layered reconciliation methodologies into an undergraduate digital methods or textual editing curriculum requires transitioning abstract mathematical theories into actionable, programmatic competencies. The modern digital editor must be trained not merely as a passive transcriber, but as an auditor of automated systems, fully capable of deploying statistical tools to quantify error, detect systemic AI bias, and establish ground truth.

A Practical Guide to Calculating CER/WER in Python

To genuinely comprehend the mechanics of CER and WER, students must look beyond black-box libraries and understand the programmatic logic of the Wagner-Fischer algorithm. In a Python-based curriculum, this dynamic programming approach can be mapped conceptually.

The process begins by defining a function that accepts a reference string (the ground truth) and a hypothesis string (the HTR output). The script must initialize a two-dimensional array (matrix) where the number of rows equals the length of the reference string plus one, and the number of columns equals the length of the hypothesis string plus one. The critical pedagogical lesson here is the extra row and column: they represent the base case of the empty string.

The initialization phase populates the first row and first column with incrementally increasing integers. This represents the accumulating mathematical cost of purely deleting characters from the reference string to eventually reach the empty string, or purely inserting characters to build the hypothesis string from nothing.

The core of the algorithm utilizes nested iterations (e.g., iterating through rows, and within that, iterating through columns) to evaluate the cost of transition for every single character pair. A conditional check determines the substitution cost: if the current character in the reference matches the current character in the hypothesis, the substitution cost is 0; if they conflict, the cost is 1. The current cell in the matrix is then assigned the absolute minimum value among three calculated paths:

  1. The cell directly above + 1 (representing a Deletion)

  2. The cell directly to the left + 1 (representing an Insertion)

  3. The cell diagonally up-and-left + the calculated substitution cost (representing a Substitution or a Match).

The terminal value located at the bottom-right corner of the matrix holds the minimum edit distance. To calculate the Character Error Rate, the student simply divides this integer by the total length of the reference string. To adapt this exact logic for the Word Error Rate, the student must first tokenize the reference and hypothesis strings by whitespace into arrays of words, and execute the exact same Wagner-Fischer matrix logic on the word arrays rather than the character arrays. By manually constructing this matrix, students visualize precisely how HTR pipelines trace and misinterpret errors, gaining the ability to write custom scripts capable of penalizing specific types of historical errors to better reflect paleographic realities.

Writing an Audit Reconciliation Report

When establishing a consensus text from mixed manual and AI modalities, the digital editor must author a formal reconciliation audit report. This document ensures epistemological transparency, recording exactly how the text moved from the raw manuscript image to the final encoded XML file. An effective audit report must contain the following structural components:

  1. Ground Truth Sampling and IAA Validation: The report must clearly define the specific subset of the corpus subjected to manual double-keying to serve as the ground truth. It must document the Inter-Annotator Agreement utilizing Krippendorff’s Alpha or Fleiss' Kappa to mathematically prove that the initial human transcribers achieved high consensus. This verifies the reliability of the ground truth data against which the AI will be benchmarked.

  2. Error Rate Benchmarking: The report must transparently declare the CER and WER for all automated outputs. Crucially, the editor must identify the divergence between the two metrics. If the CER is 4% but the WER is an anomalous 25%, the audit must explicitly identify systemic tokenization or boundary detection failures within the HTR engine.

  3. Error Typology and Systematic Bias Identification: The audit should classify the most frequent substitutions. Does the model systemically confuse the long 's' with 'f'? Does it hallucinate modern punctuation where none exists? Identifying these biases dictates the strict parameters required for the subsequent Pre-Editorial Normalization phase.

  4. Structural vs. Typographical Divergence: If the text is encoded in TEI, the report must differentiate between characters misread by the OCR/HTR and XML structural errors. A missing closing tag represents a structural failure that compromises the entire document object model, taking absolute precedence over a minor typographical substitution.

  5. LLM Parameterization and Constraints: If an LLM is utilized for post-correction, the exact few-shot prompt provided to the model must be appended to the audit report. The report must demonstrate that the LLM was explicitly instructed to preserve historically significant orthography and must include a statistical evaluation of the LLM's false-positive rate regarding unprompted, anachronistic normalizations.
