HTR vs Manual Transcription Research

The Intersection of Computational Philology and Machine Learning: Evaluating Handwritten Text Recognition and Large Language Models against Diplomatic Ground Truth

1. Abstract & Executive Summary

The digitization of global cultural heritage has catalyzed a profound paradigm shift within textual scholarship, transitioning the field from manual paleographic decipherment to computationally assisted transcription workflows. This exhaustive report investigates the efficacy of automated Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) platforms—most notably Transkribus and eScriptorium—and multimodal Large Language Models (LLMs) in generating accurate text from historical manuscripts. The core thesis substantiated herein posits that while automated HTR technologies and vision-language LLMs substantially accelerate manuscript transcription pipelines, they fundamentally require human-generated, manual diplomatic transcription as an authoritative ground truth. It is only against this meticulously encoded baseline that automated character error rates (CER), word error rates (WER), layout analysis accuracy, and algorithmically hallucinated readings can be measured, evaluated, and mitigated.

In recent years, the digital humanities have witnessed the rapid evolution of optical character recognition from Hidden Markov Models to Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs) utilizing Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss, and subsequently to Transformer-based architectures like TrOCR. Concurrently, general-purpose multimodal LLMs such as GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 2.5 Pro have demonstrated remarkable zero-shot transcription capabilities. Yet, an exhaustive analysis of empirical benchmarks across multiple centuries and scripts reveals a stark "specialization gap". While LLMs excel in modern, standardized handwriting, they frequently falter when applied to complex historical materials, exhibiting phenomena such as "over-historicization"—the systematic algorithmic insertion of archaic characters that are absent from the source document.  

Conversely, domain-specific, computationally lightweight HTR models fine-tuned on dedicated paleographic datasets consistently outperform massive LLMs in historical contexts, effectively managing the idiosyncrasies of medieval and early modern orthography. Ultimately, the successful deployment of computational philology within academic curricula and digital scholarly editions relies on a rigorous "human-in-the-loop" methodology. Within this paradigm, traditional diplomatic transcription provides the rigorous, unadulterated anchor necessary for training, evaluating, and post-correcting neural architectures, ensuring that the historical record remains free from algorithmic contamination.  

2. Technical, Methodological & Historical Foundations

Diplomatic vs. Normalized Transcription Conventions

To evaluate the output of any automated recognition system, an epistemological baseline must first be established. In the discipline of textual scholarship, this baseline is the diplomatic transcription. A diplomatic transcription seeks to reproduce the surface features of a manuscript as precisely as possible, preserving original spelling, punctuation, capitalization, word division, variant letterforms (allographs), line divisions, and unexpanded abbreviations. This methodology stands in stark contrast to normalized or interpretative transcriptions, which modernize orthography, silently expand abbreviations, and regularize punctuation to facilitate readability for contemporary audiences.  

In the digital ecosystem, diplomatic transcriptions are typically encoded using the Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) XML guidelines, which utilize an Ordered Hierarchy of Content Objects (OHCO). TEI XML allows scholars to explicitly mark the semantic and paleographic realities of the text in a machine-readable format. For instance, the TEI <choice> element is heavily utilized to encapsulate editorial decisions directly alongside the historical evidence. When encountering a scribal abbreviation, a scholarly editor wraps the manuscript's literal ink trace in an <abbr> tag and the expanded scholarly interpretation in an <expan> tag, placing both within the <choice> parent element. Similarly, original idiosyncratic spellings and their normalized, modern equivalents are encoded using the <orig> and <reg> tags. The <gap> and <unclear> elements are deployed to represent physical damage to the parchment, such as fading or holes, ensuring the transcription reflects the material condition of the artifact.  

This level of diplomatic granularity is the absolute foundation of computational philology. Neural models trained on normalized or modernized text face severe alignment issues and tend to hallucinate or "over-normalize" when encountering novel historical domains, effectively destroying the historical linguistic data. By providing a strictly diplomatic TEI XML ground truth, scholars ensure that the HTR model learns the exact mathematical correlation between the visual morphological features (the ink on the parchment) and the specific historical graphemes they represent, devoid of modern editorial intervention or silent corrections.  

Document Layout Analysis and Line/Region Segmentation

Before character recognition can occur, a machine learning pipeline must isolate the text within the digitized artifact. This process, known as Layout Analysis (LA) or Document Image Analysis, has historically been a significant bottleneck in archival processing. Platforms like Transkribus and eScriptorium (which implements the Kraken engine) approach this through a hierarchy of region detection and baseline extraction.  

Early systems relied on heuristic or semantic segmentation, attempting to define rectangular bounding boxes around paragraphs. However, historical manuscripts frequently possess highly irregular, non-rectangular layouts, including interlinear additions, marginal glosses, and complex multi-column formats. Modern HTR systems utilize advanced neural architectures—often based on fully convolutional networks or object detection frameworks like YALTAi (You Actually Look Twice At it)—to predict text baselines, which are the imaginary lines upon which characters sit. Once the baselines are accurately detected, the system calculates a polygonal envelope (a line region) around the ascenders and descenders of the text, segmenting the image into individual, processable strips.  

The accuracy of Layout Analysis is critical, as errors in segmentation directly and catastrophically cascade into the recognition phase. If the reading order is incorrectly interpreted by the layout model, or if diacritics positioned far above the baseline are truncated by an inaccurate extraction polygon, the resulting textual output will be structurally and semantically flawed, regardless of the recognition model's sophistication. In complex manuscripts, such as glossed medieval texts, distinguishing the main text column from the microscopic, heavily abbreviated marginalia remains a formidable layout challenge.  

Evolution of Neural HTR Architectures: From PyLaia to TrOCR

The architectural backbone of automated historical transcription has undergone rapid iterations over the last decade. The current academic landscape is dominated by two primary paradigms: Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs) equipped with CTC loss, and Transformer-based attention models.  

The PyLaia architecture exemplifies the CRNN-CTC paradigm and has long served as a foundational engine within the Transkribus platform. PyLaia processes a pre-segmented text-line image by passing it through a deep Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to extract a sequence of one-dimensional visual feature maps. These spatial features are subsequently fed into stacked Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) layers, a type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) that models the sequential, left-to-right dependencies of the handwriting.  

The critical mathematical innovation enabling this architecture is the Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss function. Prior to CTC, training an OCR model required pixel-perfect, character-level bounding boxes, which were economically impossible to generate for millions of historical manuscript pages. CTC calculates the marginal probability of a target sequence over all possible alignments between the input image frames and the output characters, introducing a specialized "blank" token to manage repeated characters and non-text visual noise. This alignment-free decoding eliminates the need for character-level bounding boxes, allowing models to learn directly from line-level diplomatic transcriptions paired with unsegmented line images. While highly computationally efficient and capable of running on consumer-grade hardware, CTC decoders rely heavily on local visual cues and struggle to model broader semantic or linguistic contexts, rendering them vulnerable to visual ambiguities.  

In contrast, Transformer-based architectures, such as TrOCR (Transformer-based Optical Character Recognition) and proprietary "supermodels" like Transkribus Titan, eliminate recurrent LSTM layers entirely, opting for an attention-driven approach. TrOCR utilizes a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) to encode the image into patch embeddings, and an autoregressive text Transformer to decode the characters. The cross-attention mechanisms within the decoder allow the model to dynamically attend to disparate regions of the image and the preceding textual context simultaneously. This results in robust recognition capabilities, particularly for heavily degraded text or highly variable writer styles. Adaptations such as AdapterTrOCR have further refined this by injecting domain-specific handwriting style and historical language adapters into the generic TrOCR framework, drastically reducing error rates for low-resource historical languages. However, this power comes at the cost of significantly higher computational overhead and a structural tendency to "hallucinate" plausible but incorrect textual sequences when visual evidence is severely degraded.  

Vision-Language LLMs in Historical Paleography

The most recent disruption in textual scholarship is the application of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and multimodal Large Language Models—such as OpenAI’s GPT-4o, Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro—to zero-shot optical character recognition tasks. Unlike specialized HTR models that require expensive line-level layout analysis and supervised fine-tuning on domain-specific datasets, multimodal LLMs can ingest an entire high-resolution manuscript page and output a transcription based solely on natural language prompt instructions.  

These massive models leverage billions of parameters trained on vast, heterogeneous internet corpora, granting them unparalleled zero-shot capabilities in standard contemporary scripts and dominant modern languages like English. Early studies noted that LLMs could successfully bypass the error-prone pipeline of layout analysis and line segmentation by holistically processing the page. However, their application to the digital humanities poses profound methodological challenges regarding reproducibility, temporal bias, language bias, and evaluation metrics. The "black box" nature of proprietary LLMs prevents scholars from analyzing their training data or fine-tuning their base parameters for highly specific historical scripts. This limitation necessitates a rigorous, quantitative comparative evaluation against traditional, open-source HTR systems.  

3. Comparative Methodological Evaluation

To rigorously assess the utility of these computational tools for scholarly editing, they must be benchmarked against human-generated, double-blind verified diplomatic transcriptions. Standard automated metrics include the Character Error Rate (CER)—calculated via the Levenshtein distance representing the minimum number of insertions, substitutions, and deletions required to match the machine hypothesis to the ground truth, normalized by the sequence length—and the Word Error Rate (WER).

Multimodal LLMs demonstrate formidable capabilities on modern, standardized handwriting, particularly in English. On datasets such as the IAM database (contemporary English handwriting), proprietary models have achieved CERs that rival specialized systems.  

However, when applied to historical documents, low-resource languages, and non-standard orthography, the performance of general-purpose LLMs degrades precipituously.

Over-Historicization and Temporal Hallucination

When multimodal LLMs are applied to historical documents, they exhibit unique failure modes not present in standard CRNN-CTC pipelines. Chief among these is "over-historicization," a phenomenon identified in comprehensive studies of 18th-century Russian Civil font manuscripts. Rather than merely struggling to recognize characters, LLMs actively hallucinate archaic, pre-Petrine Church Slavonic graphemes (e.g., the little yus [ѧ], omega [ѡ], or ot [ѿ]) that had been deliberately eliminated from the specific historical period being analyzed.  

To quantify this temporal bias, researchers have introduced two novel historical evaluation metrics to complement CER:

  1. Historical Character Preservation Rate (HCPR): Measures the proportion of period-specific characters accurately preserved by the model, isolating a model's tendency to silently modernize text against the rules of diplomatic transcription.  

  2. Archaic Insertion Rate (AIR): Tracks the frequency with which a model hallucinates anachronistic characters into the transcript.

Characteristic Error Distributions: Ligatures, Abbreviations, and Damage

Beyond metric percentages, the qualitative nature of errors differs fundamentally between architectures, necessitating different human-in-the-loop correction strategies.

  • PyLaia (CTC Architectures): Errors in CTC models are highly localized and visually driven. PyLaia struggles with heavily abbreviated words or dense ligatures where the visual footprint of characters overlaps entirely, as it attempts to map discrete visual slices to discrete outputs. It rarely hallucinates entire words based on context; instead, it frequently produces orthographically impossible sequences (e.g., substituting 'e' for 'c' in a complex string) if the visual evidence is ambiguous.  

  • TrOCR and Transformer Architectures: Because these models possess strong internal language models via their autoregressive decoders, their errors often manifest as plausible but entirely incorrect words. They exhibit an "over-willingness to guess," which artificially drives down WER but can increase CER. This renders the output particularly dangerous for digital editors, as the hallucinated word makes semantic sense but does not match the visual ink trace, making errors harder for human proofreaders to spot during collation.  

  • Multimodal LLMs: Suffer from prompt saturation, formatting hallucinations, and unreliability in handling physical damage (e.g., bleed-through, faded ink). While an LLM may correctly read the visible text, it frequently fails to adhere to the strict line-break preservation required by diplomatic guidelines, or it may inject external contextual metadata directly into the transcription block.  

4. Real-World Digital Edition Case Studies

To contextualize these architectural nuances and methodological debates, an in-depth examination of specific paleographic deployments in Greek and Latin manuscript traditions is highly instructive.

Case Study 1: Byzantine Greek Manuscripts and Polytonic Complexities

Research conducted by Elpida Perdiki on the manuscript tradition of John Chrysostom provides a profound look into the challenges of Byzantine Greek handwritten text recognition. Greek manuscripts present highly unique visual and linguistic challenges: the script heavily utilizes scripta continua (continuous text without word boundaries), vast networks of enclitics, complex hanging lines, and a polytonic accentuation system featuring at least five distinct accent characters and multiple combinations thereof.  

Initial tests using traditional CLI-based open-source tools proved computationally and technically inaccessible for many humanities scholars; however, the Transkribus graphical user interface permitted rapid, iterative training. A critical finding of this project was the extreme efficacy of transfer learning and mixed-model training across massive archives. By training mixed HTR models across multiple manuscripts originating from different repositories (e.g., Mount Athos, the Vatican, Paris), researchers discovered that the required volume of manual ground truth per individual manuscript could be reduced by up to 80% without sacrificing accuracy.  

Despite these successes, errors remained localized around the specific paleographic features of Greek. Automated layout analysis frequently omitted accents, breathings, or suffix abbreviations placed high above the line. To achieve a CER below 10%, researchers were forced to manually adjust line extraction polygons during the ground truth phase to ensure the visual capture of these vital diacritics. Furthermore, the presence of scripta continua fundamentally confused standard tokenizer metrics. The HTR system would correctly recognize the physical characters but fuse them into meaningless tokens (e.g., recognizing the distinct words "ἀλλὰ τί" as the single token "ἀλλατι"), necessitating the development of entirely separate downstream NLP error-correction modules based on ancient Greek lexicons.  

Case Study 2: Medieval Latin and the CATMuS Specialization Gap

The digitization of medieval Latin charters and manuscripts reveals the severe limitations of scaling laws when applied to historical documents. The CATMuS (Consistent Approaches to Transcribing Manuscripts) project aggregated a diachronic dataset spanning the 8th to 16th centuries, comprising over 160,000 lines of text and 5 million characters. This dataset was designed to enforce a uniform framework for semi-diplomatic transcription, balancing visual diversity by merging variant representations of characters while preserving crucial scribal abbreviations.  

Researchers utilized the massive CATMuS dataset to fine-tune a Transformer model, resulting in the domain-specific TrOCR-Medieval-Base. In a rigorous benchmarking exercise against massive general-purpose VLMs, the domain-tuned TrOCR-Medieval-Base achieved a highly functional CER of 14.6%. In staggering contrast, DeepSeek-OCR scored 63.0% and Mistral-OCR-3 scored 84.7% on the exact same medieval Latin samples.

The BullingerDB dataset—comprising the 16th-century correspondence of Heinrich Bullinger in Latin and Early New High German—serves as an expansive benchmark for multilingual, multi-author handwritten text recognition. Across 20,898 pages written by nearly 796 distinct writers over six decades, the stylistic and paleographic variance is immense.  

Applying standard CRNN architectures to this chaotic data yielded only moderate results, but the implementation of TrOCR demonstrated the power of transformer attention mechanisms for highly variable scripts, achieving a leading CER of 9.1% across the entire corpus. Furthermore, efforts to improve the pipeline utilized a novel self-training method based on CTC alignment algorithms to match full diplomatic transcriptions directly to line images. This technique mitigated human errors in hyphenation annotations and drove the PyLaia CER down by an additional 1.1%. This case underscores that regardless of the neural architecture deployed, the hygiene, absolute size, and paleographic diversity of the training dataset dictate the ultimate upper bound of model performance.  

5. Pedagogical Takeaways & Synthesis for Digital Editors

The integration of computational philology into undergraduate and graduate digital humanities curricula, as well as the daily workflows of scholarly editors, necessitates a rigorous, structured methodological framework. The allure of automated, zero-shot AI transcriptions must be tempered by a deep understanding of neural mechanics and the absolute, unyielding requirement for scholarly human intervention.

Curricular Integration of TEI XML and HTR Workflows

A modern digital editing curriculum must embed a "human-in-the-loop" philosophy from the very first syllabus. Students must be taught that HTR technology does not replace the paleographer; rather, it amplifies them. The pedagogical sequence should necessarily begin with manual training in strict diplomatic transcription using TEI XML. Understanding how to encode scribal abbreviations (<choice>, <abbr>, <expan>), structural layout shifts (<pb>, <lb>), and damaged text (<gap>, <unclear>) forces the student to confront the physical materiality of the manuscript.  

Once students master the creation of high-fidelity ground truth, they can transition to platforms like Transkribus or eScriptorium. Here, they must learn the operational realities of layout analysis—manually correcting baselines and polygons to ensure diacritics, ascenders, and descenders are fully captured—before attempting to train a custom model. This workflow effectively demystifies AI, framing it not as an infallible oracle, but as a statistical engine that requires perfectly curated data to function.  

Guidelines for Training and Model Selection

When approaching a newly digitized archival collection, digital editors and students should adhere to the following best practices:

  1. Transfer Learning is Essential: Never train a model from scratch if a foundational model exists. Fine-tuning a robust base model (e.g., Transkribus Titan, or TrOCR-Medieval-Base) on as few as 50 to 100 pages of newly created ground truth can rapidly drop the CER to usable levels, saving hundreds of hours of computational and human labor.  

  2. Dataset Diversity over Volume: When selecting pages for ground truth, do not merely transcribe the first 50 consecutive pages of a codex. Neural networks struggle heavily with out-of-vocabulary terms and sudden stylistic shifts. Sample pages chronologically, thematically, and across multiple scribes to expose the model to the maximum variance in hand, ink degradation, and vocabulary.  

  3. Mind the Architecture: For highly consistent, formulaic texts written by a single scribe, a CRNN-CTC model (like PyLaia) is computationally cheap and highly effective. For heavily damaged texts, multi-author collections, or those with extreme scribal variance, Transformer models (like TrOCR or Titan) offer better contextual guessing, though editors must be highly vigilant for visually plausible but semantically hallucinated words.  

  4. Avoid General LLMs for Primary Transcription: Unless the manuscript is a 19th- or 20th-century document in a widely represented language (e.g., modern English), do not rely on zero-shot VLMs for primary diplomatic transcription. The risk of archaic insertion, silent modernization, and structural hallucination drastically outweighs the setup time saved.  

Establishing Transcription Quality Thresholds

In scholarly textual editing, perfection is the absolute goal, but computational utility requires pragmatism. Digital humanities practitioners must establish acceptable thresholds for automated output based on the project's downstream goals.

  • CER < 5% (Excellent): At this threshold, the text requires minimal human post-correction. It is suitable for direct publication in a digital edition (after a standard human proofread) and highly accurate downstream NLP tasks, such as lemmatization and POS tagging.  

  • CER 5% – 10% (Good/Usable): The text contains localized errors, often struggling with complex abbreviations, rare ligatures, or highly specific named entities. While it requires heavier manual post-editing for a critical edition, it is entirely sufficient for corpus linguistics, keyword spotting, and hierarchical document clustering.  

  • CER > 15% (Poor): At this level, the cognitive load and time required for a human to post-correct the automated text often exceeds the time required to manually transcribe it from scratch.  

Furthermore, scholars must look beyond purely quantitative metrics. While a low CER is desirable, researchers must evaluate the nature of the errors. A model that achieves a 4% CER by consistently modernizing historical spellings (resulting in a low Historical Character Preservation Rate) is fundamentally useless for a diplomatic digital edition. Conversely, a model with an 8% CER that strictly preserves paleographic ligatures and abbreviations, but struggles with faded ink, may be highly valuable and easily correctable by a trained philologist.  

The successful generation of accurate text from historical manuscripts remains inextricably bound to the labor of the textual scholar. Manual diplomatic transcription provides the vital ground truth necessary to train, anchor, and correct automated systems. By integrating these methodologies into pedagogical curricula, the next generation of digital editors can harness the immense acceleration provided by AI without sacrificing the rigorous philological fidelity owed to the historical record.

This results in highly robust recognition capabilities, particularly for heavily degraded text or complex layouts. However, this comes at the cost of significantly higher computational overhead and a tendency to "hallucinate" plausible but visually incorrect text when ink traces are ambiguous. To optimize these massive models for historical contexts, modular extensions such as AdapterTrOCR have been developed. AdapterTrOCR injects specialized domain adapters—one for historical language adaptation and another for specific handwriting style adaptation—into the generic TrOCR framework, drastically reducing error rates for low-resource languages like 16th-century Latin without requiring full-parameter fine-tuning.  

2.4 Vision-Language LLMs in Historical Paleography

The most recent disruption in textual scholarship is the application of multimodal Large Language Models (LLMs)—such as OpenAI’s GPT-4o, Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro—to zero-shot optical character recognition. Unlike specialized HTR models that require extensive line-level layout analysis and supervised fine-tuning, VLMs can ingest an entire high-resolution manuscript page and output a transcription based purely on natural language prompt instructions.  

These models leverage billions of parameters trained on vast, heterogeneous internet corpora, granting them unparalleled zero-shot capabilities in standard contemporary scripts, tabular data reconstruction, and dominant global languages. When applied to clean, modern handwriting, they often rival or exceed traditional HTR systems. However, their application to the digital humanities poses profound methodological challenges. Because scholars cannot control the pre-training data, LLMs are subject to extreme temporal biases, language skews (heavily favoring English), and unpredictable generative hallucinations. Consequently, assessing their viability requires a rigorous, metrics-driven comparative evaluation against established, specialized HTR baselines using verifiable historical ground truth.  

3. Comparative Methodological Evaluation

To rigorously assess the utility of these computational tools for scholarly editing, they must be benchmarked against human-generated, double-blind verified diplomatic transcriptions. Standard automated metrics include the Character Error Rate (CER)—the Levenshtein distance representing the minimum number of insertions, substitutions, and deletions required to match the machine hypothesis to the human ground truth, normalized by the sequence length—and the Word Error Rate (WER).  

3.1 Specialized HTR vs. Multimodal LLMs: The Specialization Gap

Extensive benchmarking reveals a bifurcated landscape where the optimal technology is highly dependent on the historical era, script, and linguistic complexity of the manuscript. Multimodal LLMs demonstrate formidable capabilities on modern, standardized handwriting. On datasets such as the IAM (contemporary English handwriting), proprietary models like GPT-4o have achieved CERs as low as 1.39%, outperforming many traditional specialized architectures operating in zero-shot conditions.  

However, when applied to historical documents, non-Latin scripts, and low-resource languages, the performance of general-purpose LLMs degrades precipitously.

Adding these complex generative steps introduces prompt saturation and cascading error propagation that severely degrades aggregate translation quality.  

3.2 Over-Historicization and Temporal Bias

When LLMs are applied to historical documents, they exhibit unique, systemic failure modes not present in standard CRNN-CTC pipelines. Chief among these is "over-historicization," a phenomenon meticulously documented in studies of 18th-century Russian texts printed in the Civil font. Rather than merely struggling to recognize characters, LLMs actively hallucinate archaic, pre-Petrine Church Slavonic graphemes (e.g., the little yus [ѧ], omega [ѡ], monograph uk [ꙋ], or ot [ѿ]) that had been explicitly eliminated from the Russian alphabet prior to the historical period being analyzed.  

To mathematically quantify this temporal bias, digital humanities scholars have introduced novel evaluation metrics tailored to historical corpus creation:

  1. Historical Character Preservation Rate (HCPR): Measures the proportion of period-specific characters accurately preserved by the model, isolating a system's tendency to silently modernize historical texts.  

  2. Archaic Insertion Rate (AIR): Tracks the frequency with which a generative model hallucinates anachronistic characters belonging to earlier historical epochs.

3.3 Characteristic Error Distributions

Beyond metric percentages, the qualitative nature of transcription errors differs fundamentally based on the underlying neural architecture.

CTC-Based Architectures (e.g., PyLaia): Because CTC maps discrete visual slices directly to discrete character outputs, its errors are highly localized to the visual evidence. It struggles significantly with heavily abbreviated words or dense medieval ligatures where the visual footprint of separate characters overlaps entirely. However, it rarely hallucinates entire words. If the visual evidence is ambiguous (e.g., faded ink, cross-outs), a CTC model will frequently produce an orthographically impossible sequence of letters rather than guessing a false word.  

Attention-Based Transformers (e.g., TrOCR): Because these models possess strong internal language modeling capabilities through cross-attention, their errors often manifest as plausible but entirely incorrect words. They exhibit an "over-willingness to guess," which artificially drives down Word Error Rates but can increase Character Error Rates. This renders the output exceptionally difficult for human editors to parse during post-correction, because the visual trace of the manuscript ink does not match the hallucinated word supplied by the language model.  

Vision-Language Models (LLMs): General-purpose LLMs suffer from prompt saturation and formatting hallucinations. They may correctly read the underlying text but routinely fail to adhere to the strict line-break preservation and structural encoding required by diplomatic guidelines. Furthermore, they are prone to injecting external contextual metadata, conversational filler, or unprompted historical summaries directly into the transcription block.  

4. Real-World Digital Edition Case Studies

To contextualize these architectural nuances, an examination of specific paleographic deployments in complex Greek and Latin manuscript traditions is highly instructive.

4.1 Byzantine Greek Manuscripts and Polytonic Complexities

Research conducted by Elpida Perdiki on the voluminous manuscript tradition of John Chrysostom provides a profound look into the challenges of Byzantine Greek handwritten text recognition. Greek manuscripts present highly specific visual challenges: the script heavily utilizes scripta continua (continuous text without discernible word boundaries), vast networks of enclitic ligatures, complex hanging lines (where characters hang below the ruled baseline rather than sitting upon it), and a polytonic accentuation system featuring at least five distinct accent characters and multiple vertical combinations thereof.  

Initial tests utilizing traditional command-line interface (CLI) tools proved computationally inaccessible for many humanities scholars; however, the Transkribus Graphical User Interface (GUI) permitted rapid iterative training and visual layout correction. A critical finding of this project was the extreme efficacy of transfer learning and mixed-model training. By training mixed HTR models across multiple manuscripts from geographically distinct libraries (e.g., Athos Dionysiou, Vatican, Paris), the required volume of manual ground truth per individual manuscript could be reduced by up to 80% without sacrificing accuracy.

4.2 Medieval Latin and the CATMuS Specialization Gap

The digitization of medieval Latin charters and manuscripts reveals the severe limitations of scaling laws when applied to historical documents. The CATMuS (Consistent Approaches to Transcribing Manuscripts) project aggregated a massive diachronic dataset spanning the 8th to 16th centuries, comprising over 160,000 lines of text across 200 manuscripts. This dataset enforces a uniform framework for semi-diplomatic transcription, balancing visual diversity by merging variant representations of characters while meticulously preserving crucial scribal abbreviations.  

Researchers utilized the CATMuS dataset to fine-tune a Transformer model, resulting in the open-source TrOCR-Medieval-Base. In a rigorous benchmarking exercise utilizing the Interpres-Parallel-Corpus (IPC)—a tripartite dataset mapping Latin manuscript images to transcriptions to expert translations—the domain-tuned TrOCR-Medieval-Base achieved a CER of 14.6%. In staggering contrast, massive commercial models failed entirely: DeepSeek-OCR scored 63.0% and Mistral-OCR-3 scored 84.7% on the exact same medieval Latin samples.  

Furthermore, within the Transkribus ecosystem, specialized Latin models have achieved remarkable success when applied to specific temporal windows. The "Carolingian Minuscule Model" (CMM), trained on standardized Carolingian texts spanning the 9th to 11th centuries, achieved an exceptional CER of 5.1% on standard manuscript pages. Yet, the limits of the HTR architecture were exposed when applied to pages containing minuscule marginal glosses. On glossed pages, the CER degraded substantially to 13.6% due to shifting vocabularies, heavier localized abbreviation usage, and reduced image resolution, demonstrating that even highly successful models remain brittle when layout topography shifts unexpectedly.  

4.3 The BullingerDB and Multilingual Epistolary Networks

The BullingerDB dataset—comprising the 16th-century correspondence of Swiss reformer Heinrich Bullinger in Latin and Early New High German—serves as an expansive benchmark for highly complex multilingual, multi-author HTR. Across 20,898 pages written by 796 distinct writers, the stylistic and paleographic variance is immense, representing a worst-case scenario for automated recognition.  

Applying standard CRNN architectures to this data yielded moderate results, but the implementation of TrOCR demonstrated the raw power of attention mechanisms for highly variable scripts, achieving a leading CER of 9.1% across the entire multi-author corpus. Furthermore, efforts to improve the pipeline utilized a novel self-training method based on CTC alignment algorithms. By matching full corpus transcriptions to text line images using dynamic programming and CTC loss probabilities, researchers were able to programmatically correct errors in human hyphenation annotations within the ground truth, thereby driving the PyLaia CER down by an additional 1.1% without requiring further manual labor. This case underscores a fundamental axiom of computational philology: regardless of the neural architecture deployed, the hygiene, size, and paleographic diversity of the underlying dataset dictate the absolute upper bound of model performance.  

5. Pedagogical Takeaways and Synthesis for Digital Editors

The integration of computational philology into digital humanities curricula and the daily workflows of scholarly editors necessitates a rigorous, empirically grounded methodological framework. The allure of automated, zero-shot AI transcriptions must be strictly tempered by an understanding of neural mechanics and the absolute requirement for scholarly intervention and validation.

5.1 Curricular Integration of TEI XML and Human-in-the-Loop Workflows

A modern digital editing curriculum must fundamentally embed a "human-in-the-loop" philosophy. Students must be taught that HTR does not replace the paleographer; rather, it amplifies them, shifting their labor from initial decipherment to high-level validation and semantic enrichment. The pedagogical sequence should necessarily begin with manual training in diplomatic transcription using TEI XML. Understanding how to structurally encode scribal abbreviations (<choice>, <abbr>, <expan>), layout shifts (<pb>, <lb>), and damaged text (<gap>, <unclear>) forces the student to confront the deep materiality of the manuscript.  

Once students master the creation of high-fidelity ground truth, they can transition to platforms like Transkribus or eScriptorium. Here, they must interact with the realities of Document Layout Analysis—manually correcting algorithmic baselines and polygonal envelopes to ensure diacritics and descenders are fully captured—before attempting to train a custom model. This workflow demystifies Artificial Intelligence, framing it not as an infallible digital oracle, but as a statistical engine that requires highly curated, domain-specific data.  

5.2 Guidelines for Training and Model Selection

When approaching a new archival collection, digital editors and researchers should adhere to the following empirically derived best practices:

  1. Transfer Learning is Essential: Never train a neural model from a random initialization if a foundational model exists. Fine-tuning a robust base model (e.g., Transkribus Titan, CMM, or TrOCR-Medieval-Base) on as few as 50 to 100 pages of newly created ground truth can rapidly drop the Character Error Rate to usable levels, drastically reducing compute time.  

  2. Dataset Diversity Supersedes Volume: When selecting manuscript pages for ground truth creation, do not merely transcribe the first 50 consecutive pages. Neural networks struggle profoundly with out-of-vocabulary terms and stylistic shifts. Sample pages chronologically, thematically, and spatially across the entire corpus to expose the model to the maximum variance in scribe hand, ink degradation, and vocabulary.  

  3. Mind the Architectural Bias: For highly consistent, formulaic texts with minimal degradation, a CRNN-CTC model (like PyLaia) is computationally cheap, highly effective, and will not hallucinate false words. For heavily damaged texts or those with extreme scribal variance, Transformer models (like TrOCR) offer superior contextual guessing, though editors must be exceptionally vigilant during post-correction for hallucinated sequences.  

  4. Avoid General LLMs for Primary Transcription: Unless the manuscript is a late 19th- or 20th-century document in a widely represented language (e.g., modern English), do not rely on zero-shot VLMs for diplomatic transcription. The high risk of archaic insertion, silent modernization, and structural hallucination vastly outweighs the initial setup time saved.  

5.3 Establishing Transcription Quality Thresholds

In scholarly textual editing, perfection is the absolute goal, but computational utility requires pragmatism. Digital humanities practitioners must establish acceptable quantitative thresholds for automated output based on the project's specific downstream goals.

  • CER < 5% (Excellent): At this threshold, the text requires minimal human post-correction. It is suitable for direct publication in a semi-diplomatic digital edition (following a standard proofread) and is highly accurate for downstream NLP tasks such as lemmatization, Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging, and Named Entity Recognition.  

  • CER 5% – 10% (Good/Usable): The text contains localized errors, frequently struggling with complex abbreviations, rare ligatures, or specialized punctuation. While it requires heavier manual post-editing to achieve the standards of a critical edition, it is entirely sufficient for internal corpus linguistics, keyword spotting, and hierarchical document clustering.  

  • CER > 15% (Poor): At this level, the cognitive load and temporal investment required for a human editor to post-correct the automated text often exceed the time required to manually transcribe the document from scratch via keystroke.  

Furthermore, scholars must evaluate the qualitative nature of the errors, not merely the quantitative percentage. A general LLM that achieves a 4% CER by consistently modernizing historical spellings (resulting in a low Historical Character Preservation Rate) is fundamentally useless for a diplomatic digital edition. Conversely, a CTC model with an 8% CER that strictly preserves paleographic ligatures but struggles with faded marginalia may remain a highly valuable asset to the philologist.
