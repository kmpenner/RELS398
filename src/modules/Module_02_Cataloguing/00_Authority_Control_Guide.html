Authority Control — Finding the Registries That Actually Govern Your Document

Authority control means identifying your object in the shared registries that scholarship uses to disambiguate books, manuscripts, people, and places. The specific registries differ by tradition, and choosing the wrong one is a methodological error, not a harmless one.

Start with the general-purpose layer, which applies to almost everything:

  • VIAF (viaf.org) — international authority file for names (authors, scribes, printers, recipients). Aggregates national library authorities.
  • Wikidata (wikidata.org) — structured identifiers for persons, works, places, and institutions. Use the Q number.
  • Library of Congress authorities (id.loc.gov) — LCCN and name/title authorities; strong for printed material and American imprints.
  • GeoNames / GeoNames-style place registries — for the <origPlace> and <settlement> values in your header.

Then the tradition-specific layer. Here is where your three documents diverge:

Olyvia
French decree, 1791		 Read the date as the pivot it is. 1791 sits after 1789, and the Archives nationales splits its two reading rooms at that year: Paris (Caran) holds public archives antérieures à 1789, and Pierrefitte-sur-Seine holds those from after 1789. So this document belongs to Pierrefitte, and — unlike the pre-1789 case, where the official rule and the série Y finding aid disagree — here both agree.
Archives nationales cote. Revolutionary legislation sits in série D. The relevant sub-series is the Comité des décrets, procés-verbaux et archives (1789–an II), cotes D/I§1/1–D/I§1/39, physically at Pierrefitte. Items are described by year and month within a dossier, e.g. “Année 1791 (janvier–septembre), 65 p.” with pieces numbered by month. Cite the current form: D/I§1/1 dossier 7 bis pièce 1. Older printed guides write cotes differently (D/XXX/1 pièce 1) — if you meet the old style, give both.
Genre vocabulary changes completely in 1791. The Ancien Régime terms (édit, ordonnance, déclaration) and their material tells (green wax on silk laces, yellow wax on a double queue) describe royal letters patent and do not apply to a 1791 act. The Revolutionary instrument is the décret of the Assemblée nationale, and under the constitution of 1791 a décret required the king's sanction to become law. The finding aid's own language reflects this: it lists “relevés des décrets sanctionnés par le roi” and “actes du Corps législatif”. Work out for your document whether it is a décret, a décret sanctionné, or a loi — the distinction is substantive, not cosmetic.
Gallica / BnF ARK identifiers — the form ark:/12148/<identifier>. ARK (Archival Resource Key) is a persistent identifier: it names the object, not its current URL, so it survives the site being reorganised. BnF's prefix is 12148. Note that Gallica's Actes royaux collection is organised by reign and tapers off with the monarchy, so it is a weaker starting point for 1791 than for earlier material — search the décret collections directly.
FranceArchives as the cross-institutional portal (FRAN_IR_ for finding aids, FRAN_NP_ for authority records). The série D finding aid above is itself FRAN_IR_002914.
Not applicable here: CALAMES describes archives and manuscripts of French higher-education libraries. It is not a source for Archives nationales legislative records, so do not cite a CALAMES identifier for this project. (Recording a registry you checked and found irrelevant is fine — just do not present it as authority for your object.)
Dating: France had used the Gregorian calendar since December 1582, so no Julian day-shift applies. The Ancien Régime year-start style trap does not affect 1791 either — the year had begun on 1 January since the édit de Roussillon took full effect in 1567. The dating trap that does matter for this era is the Republican calendar: an I began 22 September 1792, the calendar was in force from 6 October 1793, and it was abolished from 1 January 1806. A document of 1791 predates it, but if your object turns out to be a later copy or a retrospective register entry, the dates may be given in both eras.
Two traps in the material evidence. (1) A watermark dates the paper, not the document. It gives you a terminus post quem at best — stock could sit unused for years. (2) From 1742 a watermark or countermark was compulsory on French paper, so its absence after that date is itself informative.

The rule that makes this gradeable: name the registries that apply, record identifiers in the exact syntax you found them, and state why each registry governs your document. If you check a standard registry and find no record for your object, that is a legitimate and creditable finding — provided you show the search you made. A verified absence beats a padded citation list.

A trap you must not fall into: some registries return “success” for identifiers that do not exist.

Verified 2026-09-10: https://viaf.org/viaf/999999999999 and https://viaf.org/viaf/12345678 both return HTTP 200 and load a page that looks like a normal VIAF record. Nothing about the response tells you the identifier is fabricated.

So do not validate an identifier by checking whether the link loads. A live link is not evidence. To confirm an authority record, open it and read the content: does the name, dates, and description actually match the person or object you mean? For an ARK, confirm the record's own metadata matches your document rather than trusting that the URL resolves.

This is the same lesson as the LLM audit. A schema can pass well-formed nonsense, and a registry can serve a URL for an identifier that means nothing. Both cases are “well-formed but false,” and both are caught only by a human checking the content against an independent source. If you catch one of these and write it up, that is exactly the critical thinking this workshop rewards.
