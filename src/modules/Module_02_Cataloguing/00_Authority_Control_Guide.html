Authority Control — Finding the Registries That Actually Govern Your Document

Authority control means identifying your object in the shared registries that scholarship uses to disambiguate books, manuscripts, people, and places. The specific registries differ by tradition, and choosing the wrong one is a methodological error, not a harmless one.

Start with the general-purpose layer, which applies to almost everything:

  • VIAF (viaf.org) — international authority file for names (authors, scribes, printers, recipients). Aggregates national library authorities.
  • Wikidata (wikidata.org) — structured identifiers for persons, works, places, and institutions. Use the Q number.
  • Library of Congress authorities (id.loc.gov) — LCCN and name/title authorities; strong for printed material and American imprints.
  • GeoNames / GeoNames-style place registries — for the <origPlace> and <settlement> values in your header.

Then the tradition-specific layer. Here is where your three documents diverge:

Olyvia
French decree		 Archives nationales cotes (shelfmarks), cited by series letter and number. Punctuation varies by series: most use a double slash (Y//9498), but series V uses a space (V1 1 à 541). Copy the form you actually find — do not normalise it.
Do not assume a reading room. The official description places public archives pre-1789 at Paris (Caran) and post-1789 at Pierrefitte-sur-Seine — but at least one series finding aid (Y, Châtelet de Paris) itself names Pierrefitte. The two sources conflict and neither has been reconciled. Confirm your specific cote with the SIV cote-checker before you state a location. Reporting an unresolved conflict is better scholarship than picking the tidier answer.
Gallica / BnF ARK identifiers — the form ark:/12148/<identifier>. ARK (Archival Resource Key) is a persistent identifier: it names the object, not its current URL, so it survives the site being reorganised. BnF's prefix is 12148. Gallica also has a browsable Actes royaux collection covering the 13th–19th centuries, arranged by reign — a good place to survey candidate documents.
FranceArchives as the cross-institutional portal (FRAN_IR_ for finding aids, FRAN_NP_ for authority records).
Not applicable here: CALAMES describes archives and manuscripts of French higher-education libraries. It is not a source for Ancien Régime royal acts, so do not cite a CALAMES identifier for this project. (Recording a registry you checked and found irrelevant is fine — just do not present it as authority for your object.)
Watch the genre vocabulary — naming yours correctly is the authority work: an ordonnance is a general law; an édit is a law on a particular object; a déclaration is a circumstantial, interpretative act. Material clues (late Ancien Régime): édits/ordonnances carry the great seal in green wax with silk laces and are not day-dated; déclarations use yellow wax and are day-dated. These conventions hardened only late in the period — do not apply them confidently before ~1650.
Dating: France had used the Gregorian calendar since December 1582, so no Julian day-shift applies to 18th-century French documents. The real trap is year-start style: for dates falling between 1 January and Easter, the modern year is often +1, because the year was reckoned from Easter in some usages until the édit de Roussillon (1564) took full effect in 1567.
A verified starting point: the Recueil des édits, arrêts, lettres patentes, déclarations, règlemens et ordonnances imprimés à Lille, année 1771 (ark:/12148/bpt6k9690620k, BnF shelfmark F-21718, 67 pieces in one in-4° volume, 108 digitised views). Its own title names six genre terms side by side, which makes it a good object for practising the distinctions above. Metadata is available machine-readably through its IIIF manifest.
Two traps in the material evidence. (1) A watermark dates the paper, not the document. It gives you a terminus post quem at best — paper could sit in a shop for years before being written on. (2) Do not treat édit perpétuel as a genre: it is a standing formula of style, and legally defeasible (the 1685 revocation of the Edict of Nantes is the proof). From 1742 a watermark or countermark was compulsory on French paper, so its absence after that date is itself informative.

The rule that makes this gradeable: name the registries that apply, record identifiers in the exact syntax you found them, and state why each registry governs your document. If you check a standard registry and find no record for your object, that is a legitimate and creditable finding — provided you show the search you made. A verified absence beats a padded citation list.

A trap you must not fall into: some registries return “success” for identifiers that do not exist.

Verified 2026-09-10: https://viaf.org/viaf/999999999999 and https://viaf.org/viaf/12345678 both return HTTP 200 and load a page that looks like a normal VIAF record. Nothing about the response tells you the identifier is fabricated.

So do not validate an identifier by checking whether the link loads. A live link is not evidence. To confirm an authority record, open it and read the content: does the name, dates, and description actually match the person or object you mean? For an ARK, confirm the record's own metadata matches your document rather than trusting that the URL resolves.

This is the same lesson as the LLM audit. A schema can pass well-formed nonsense, and a registry can serve a URL for an identifier that means nothing. Both cases are “well-formed but false,” and both are caught only by a human checking the content against an independent source. If you catch one of these and write it up, that is exactly the critical thinking this workshop rewards.
